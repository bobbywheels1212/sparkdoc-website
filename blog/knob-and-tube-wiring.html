Knob and Tube Wiring: What It Is, Insurance, and Rewiring

Knob-and-tube wiring is the open copper-and-porcelain system in the oldest houses. Insurers ask about it, and homeowners hear the worst version of the story. Here is the plain version, including when rewiring is the right next step.

Quick answer

Knob-and-tube is an early wiring method: copper on ceramic knobs, no ground wire, and insulation that is often brittle now. It is not automatically an emergency in every house. Condition, insulation coverage, and what the circuits are being asked to carry decide whether you rewire now or plan it. An assessment is how you find out.

What you are looking at

From the late 1800s through about the 1940s, houses were wired with individual copper conductors held off the wood by white ceramic knobs. Where a wire passed through a stud, it ran inside a ceramic tube. That is the name. You usually see it in an unfinished attic or a basement, in the oldest parts of Olathe and in other early Johnson County houses, not in a 1990s subdivision.

There is no equipment-grounding conductor. Original splices were often twisted, soldered, and left in the open, which was accepted practice then. Modern wiring makes those joints inside boxes. The insulation was rubber and cloth. A lot of it is brittle now. Bend it, and it cracks.

The wires were meant to shed heat into open air. Piling attic insulation on top of them traps that heat. Later owners also tapped new lights and outlets onto the old conductors. Those additions are where a lot of the real problems sit.

Insurance, and what "dangerous" actually means

Insurers ask because the wiring has no ground, the insulation is old, and the circuits were never intended for space heaters, window air conditioners, and microwaves. Many companies will surcharge a policy, limit it, or require replacement before they bind coverage. Some will still insure a house after an electrician documents the condition. We do not speak for your carrier. If you have a letter, bring it.

Fear-mongering is not useful here. Knob-and-tube that is untouched, not buried, and still flexible is a known quantity, and it can be left in place while you plan, especially if the heavy loads have already been moved to new circuits. Knob-and-tube that is crumbly, spliced in the open with tape, or buried under a foot of insulation is a different house. We will tell you which one you have. We will not tell you that every old wire is a fire today.

A GFCI receptacle can add shock protection on an ungrounded circuit. It does not restore insulation. Do not cover the wiring with insulation to "tidy the attic," and do not extend it for a remodel.

Rewiring, partial or full

Rewiring replaces those conductors with modern cable, adds grounding on the new circuits, and includes a permit when the city requires one. In Olathe that is the City of Olathe. Kitchens, baths, and laundry areas are the usual first circuits, because that is where modern loads and water meet old wire. A full rewire is the right scope when the old wiring is everywhere and in poor condition, or when a remodel has the walls open anyway.

Cloth-insulated cable is a later method that people confuse with this one. If you see a cloth-jacketed cable rather than single wires on porcelain, read cloth-insulated wiring. The service page for the work is electrical rewiring. Local permits and neighborhoods: electrician in Olathe, KS.

Common Questions

Knob-and-tube questions

What is knob-and-tube wiring?
It is an early wiring method, used from the late 1800s through about the 1940s, and occasionally a little later. Single copper conductors run on ceramic knobs and pass through ceramic tubes in the framing. There is no equipment ground wire.
Does knob-and-tube have to be removed immediately?
Not in every house. Condition matters. Wire that is intact, still in open air, and lightly loaded is a different situation from brittle wire buried in insulation with later splices added. An assessment tells you which one you have. Covering knob-and-tube with insulation, or extending it for a new circuit, is not a good idea.
Why do insurance companies ask about it?
Many carriers surcharge a policy, limit it, or ask for replacement before they will bind coverage on active knob-and-tube. Not every company has the same rule. If you have a letter, the practical path is an assessment and, when the wire is in poor condition or the carrier requires it, a rewire.
Will a GFCI fix knob-and-tube?
A GFCI can reduce shock risk on an ungrounded circuit. It does not repair cracked insulation, and it does not make the wiring safe to bury in insulation. It is not a substitute for a rewire when the wire itself is deteriorated.
What does rewiring involve?
We replace the old conductors with modern cable, add grounding where the new wiring goes, and pull a permit when the city requires one. Some houses need the whole system done. Some need the kitchen, the baths, and the attic first. We say which, after we look.
